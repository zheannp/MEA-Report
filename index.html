The Efficient Market Hypothesis (EMH)

FOUNDATIONAL THEORY
CORE DEFINITION

"Financial markets are efficient, meaning that security prices quickly and accurately reflect all available information, making it virtually impossible to consistently earn abnormal risk-adjusted returns."

Pioneered by Eugene Fama (1965)

Universal Information

Assumes information is widely and instantaneously shared across all market participants without cost.

Rapid Price Adjustment

Prices adjust so rapidly to new data that no investor can buy undervalued or sell overvalued assets predictably.

Efficient Market Hypothesis SLIDE 03 / 13

Three Forms of Market Efficiency

DEGREES OF EFFICIENCY
LEVEL 01

Weak-Form

Prices reflect all past trading information (historical prices and trading volume).

Implication: Technical analysis cannot generate excess returns.

LEVEL 02

Semi-Strong Form

Prices reflect all publicly available information (financial statements, news, macroeconomic data).

Implication: Neither fundamental nor technical analysis yields alpha.

LEVEL 03

Strong-Form

Prices reflect all information, including private insider disclosures and public data.

Implication: Even insider knowledge cannot consistently beat the market.

Fama's Market Efficiency Framework SLIDE 04 / 13

The EMH Academic Debate

SUPPORTERS VS. CRITICS

Arguments Supporting EMH

  • • Empirical Evidence: Active fund managers consistently underperform passive benchmark indices long-term.
  • • Rational Actors: Aligned with classic economic theory assuming rational wealth maximization.
  • • Fair Information Flow: Rapid dissemination limits mispricing windows.

Criticisms & Counter-Evidence

  • • Behavioral Biases: Herd behavior, emotions, and irrational exuberance drive price deviations.
  • • Persistent Anomalies: Proven size, value, and momentum factors generate systematic excess returns.
  • • Bubbles & Crashes: Tech Bubble (2000) & 2008 Crash prove extreme market inefficiencies.
Empirical & Theoretical Evaluation SLIDE 05 / 13

Practical Investment Implications

PORTFOLIO STRATEGY
01

Passive vs. Active Investing

Supports passive low-cost indexing (ETFs) over active stock picking due to high transaction fees and low probability of beating the market.

02

Market Timing Inefficacy

Attempts to time entry/exit points incur friction costs and taxes without providing predictable excess returns.

03

Diversification Imperative

Because outperformance is random, diversification across uncorrelated asset classes is the primary tool for risk reduction.

04

Illiquid Market Alpha

Active management strategies are best reserved for less liquid or under-researched asset classes where inefficiencies linger.

Overview of Behavioral Finance

PSYCHOLOGY & MARKETS
CORE PARADIGM SHIFT

Challenging Rationality

Behavioral finance combines psychology and financial economics to explain why investors make systematically irrational financial decisions.

Rational Actors → Human Psychology

Cognitive Biases

Mental shortcuts (heuristics) that lead to systematic processing errors and valuation distortions.

Emotional Drivers

Fear and greed trigger panic selling during downturns and speculative bubbles during market euphoria.

Herding & Framing

Investors follow crowds blindly, and choices vary depending on how information is presented.

Behavioral Finance Foundations SLIDE 07 / 13

Key Cognitive Biases & Heuristics

BEHAVIORAL TAXONOMY

Anchoring & Confirmation

Fixating on arbitrary initial numbers (e.g., purchase price) and seeking only confirming evidence.

Overconfidence

Overestimating personal predictive ability and control, leading to excessive risk taking.

Loss Aversion

Prospect Theory: The psychological pain of a loss is twice as intense as the pleasure of an equal gain.

Mental Accounting

Separating money into irrational mental buckets (e.g., spending windfalls foolishly vs. regular income).

Psychological Drivers of Mispricing SLIDE 08 / 13

Strengths & Limitations of Behavioral Finance

METHODOLOGY EVALUATION

Key Strengths

  • Realistic Model: Reflects actual human decision-making rather than idealized economic rationales.
  • Explains Anomalies: Provides clear psychological underpinnings for market crashes, bubbles, and momentum.
  • Improved Risk Controls: Helps investors recognize individual cognitive biases to avoid emotional errors.

Limitations

  • Lack of Predictive Power: Excellent at explaining past events, but struggles to predict timing of future anomalies.
  • No Unified Theory: Represents a collection of isolated biases rather than a single cohesive financial model.
  • Investor Heterogeneity: Biases vary significantly among individual and institutional investors.

Market Anomalies: Core Concepts

EMPIRICAL DEVIATIONS
DEFINITION

What is a Market Anomaly?

A persistent, statistically significant pricing pattern where asset returns deviate from fair value or standard risk-adjusted EMH predictions.

Challenges Weak & Semi-Strong EMH

Transient Nature

Once identified and publicly exploited by traders, many anomalies self-correct and diminish over time.

Primary Drivers

Caused by behavioral biases, transaction costs, short-selling limits, and structural market frictions.

Market Anomalies Introduction SLIDE 10 / 13

Categories of Market Anomalies

CATEGORIZATION
01. Price-Based

Momentum & PEAD

Post-Earnings Announcement Drift (PEAD): Stocks continue drifting in the direction of earnings surprises for extended periods.

02. Calendar

January Effect

Small-cap stocks systematically outperform in January due to tax-loss harvesting sell-offs in December followed by repurchases.

03. Fundamental

Value & Size Effect

Low P/E or low P/B stocks (value) and small-cap firms consistently beat high-valuation large-cap equities risk-adjusted.

04. Microstructure

Bid-Ask & Clustering

Price clustering around round numbers and bid-ask spread inefficiencies creating friction-based arbitrage constraints.

Empirical Anomaly Taxonomies SLIDE 11 / 13

Structural & Informational Classifications

THEORETICAL CLASSIFICATION

Informational Anomalies

Stem from asymmetric information processing or delayed public dissemination.

  • • Earnings surprise reversals
  • • Corporate M&A announcements
  • • Seasonal info gaps

Behavioral Anomalies

Emerge from investor psychological biases contradicting market rationality.

  • • Overreaction / Underreaction
  • • Herd-driven bubble formations
  • • Sentiment swings

Structural Anomalies

Arise from market mechanics, legal rules, and institutional friction constraints.

  • • Short-sale restrictions
  • • Regulatory trading halts
  • • Cross-border arbitrage rules

Chapter Summary & Key Takeaways

FINAL RECAP
01

EMH as a Benchmark

Markets are highly competitive and generally efficient, making passive indexing the most sensible core strategy for most investors.

02

Behavioral Reality

Human psychology and cognitive biases lead to real market inefficiencies, creating temporal pricing anomalies.

03

Exploitation Limits

Transaction costs, risks, and arbitrage barriers mean that turning anomalies into guaranteed profit is extremely difficult.
